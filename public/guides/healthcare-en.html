01 · PRIMARY CARE

Everyday care handles eight problems out of ten

Colds and fever, stomach upsets, chronic disease follow-up, vaccinations and screening all fall under primary care. Singapore has more than two thousand GP clinics and 26 government polyclinics, which between them handle the vast majority of everyday consultations.

Getting there
No appointment needed — just walk in; some clinics also take online bookings
Wait time
Usually short; many neighbourhood clinics see you within half an hour
Cost
Consultation plus medication is about S$30 to S$100+. Nights and public holidays cost more, and you're typically given three days of medication
Opening hours
Mostly office hours, some open Saturday mornings, and a few run 24 hours
Doctor
Usually the same doctor each time, which helps once they know you
Best for
Anyone who wants to be seen quickly. If you have company group insurance, check your insurer's panel clinic list first — you can only claim smoothly at clinics on that list

How to find a GP clinic

If you have group insurance, get the panel list from your insurer's app or from HR. If you don't, search Google for "GP clinic" plus your neighbourhood, or use the official HealthHub directory to find the nearest one.

Getting there
Booking required, via the HealthHub app or the cluster websites; at peak times you may wait a few days
Wait time
On-site waits are long — set aside half a day
Cost
Government-set pricing: from about S$17.50 for citizens, about S$35 for PRs and about S$75 for foreigners, with cheaper medication than private clinics
Opening hours
Longer opening hours; many open evenings and weekends
Doctor
Rostered, so you may see a different doctor each visit
Best for
Long-term chronic care, screening and vaccinations, and anyone who wants a subsidised referral to a public specialist

Worth knowing

There are currently 26 polyclinics island-wide across three clusters — SingHealth, NHG and NUP — with the government planning to expand to 32 by 2030. Beyond consultations they also handle minor procedures, maternal and child health, and chronic disease screening.

02 · HOSPITALS

Hospitals: public or private

Singapore has 11 public acute hospitals and 9 private ones. Public hospitals have first-rate facilities and subsidies but come with queues; private hospitals are faster and more comfortable but cost considerably more. Which you choose comes down to how urgent the condition is and what your insurance covers.

What public hospitals are like

Citizens and PRs receive government subsidies, and wards are graded A, B1, B2 and C. The national specialty centres sit within the public system, which is where the strongest capability for complex and serious conditions lies. The trade-off is long waits for specialist clinics and elective surgery, and under the rostering system you usually can't request a particular doctor.

What private hospitals are like

Specialist appointments are available the same day to within a week, you can request a specific doctor, and rooms are mostly single or double, so the experience and privacy are better. There are no government subsidies, so you generally need private hospitalisation insurance to cover the cost. With the right plan in place, most admissions and discharges can be handled without paying up front.

03 · SPECIALIST

Two routes to a specialist

When a condition needs specialist care you can take the subsidised route or the fast route. The difference comes down to time and money.

Subsidised route (public)

See a polyclinic doctor first for a referral to a public hospital specialist. Citizens and PRs pay about S$30 to S$60 per visit, but popular specialties can mean waits of weeks to months, you can't request a doctor, and admission defaults to a B2 or C class ward.

Fast route (private, or private clinics inside public hospitals)

Have a GP help you book, or approach the specialist directly, and be seen within a day to a week with your choice of senior doctor. A first consultation starts at about S$120 to S$250 with no government subsidy, which suits those with private insurance or a time-sensitive condition.

The ten national specialty centres

Singapore has national specialty centres for cancer, cardiology, ophthalmology, dermatology, neuroscience, infectious diseases and dentistry, representing the highest level of care available locally.

04 · EMERGENCY

A&E: when to go

A&E is for life-threatening situations. If any of the following appear, don't hesitate — get medical help immediately.

Red flags: call 995 immediately

Severe chest pain Difficulty breathing Uncontrolled bleeding Sudden confusion Sudden dizziness, numbness or weakness Sudden vision changes Coughing or vomiting blood Persistent vomiting or diarrhoea
995
Ambulance hotline · free for genuine emergencies

When you call 995, give the address, postal code, floor and unit number, whether a card is needed for the lift, and the patient's condition. Where possible, have someone who speaks English make the call.

An ambulance rule change many people miss

Since April 2019, SCDF ambulances no longer convey non-emergency cases. If the call operator assesses your situation as non-urgent, they will advise you to make your own way to a clinic. If you are conveyed and the case is later judged non-emergency, a charge of S$274 per trip applies. For non-emergency transport, contact a private ambulance operator licensed by the Ministry of Health, or simply take a taxi.

A&E triages by severity, not by arrival order

P1

Critical

Cardiac arrest, heart attack, major trauma with heavy bleeding, shock. Resuscitated immediately.

P2

Major emergency

Serious fractures, severe abdominal pain. Stable but unable to walk; assessed and treated on a trolley.

P3

Minor

Sprains, minor injuries, fever and vomiting, mild headache. Able to walk, and the wait can be long.

P4

Non-emergency

Old injuries and long-standing problems. A&E is not the right place — see a GP.

A&E attendance fees

Public hospitals charge a flat A&E attendance fee. After adjustments from the second half of 2025 this currently sits at roughly S$145 to S$190, covering basic investigations; specific procedures such as CT scans or suturing are charged separately. Private hospital A&E attendance runs about S$130 to S$200. Check each hospital's website for exact figures.

05 · MORE OPTIONS

TCM and community hospitals

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM)

Locals commonly turn to TCM for chronic conditioning, general low-level ill health, recovery periods and as a complement to Western medicine — acupuncture to ease chemotherapy side effects, postnatal recovery, or neck, shoulder, back and leg pain. A first consultation costs about S$15 to S$80; you can book online, by app or by phone, and walk-ins are usually accepted.

One thing to note: to claim TCM on company group insurance you need a clinic on the group panel list. If you're claiming TCM after an accident under a personal accident policy, a non-panel clinic will need the practitioner to complete an extra questionnaire — message me and I'll send you the form.

Community hospitals

Patients discharged from an acute hospital who still need rehabilitation, nursing or monitoring move to a community hospital for the interim, receiving physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech therapy and similar care; older patients typically stay two to four weeks. Admission requires a referral from an acute hospital doctor, followed by a means test to determine subsidy eligibility. Daily bills for citizens run about S$136 to S$409, and up to S$250 a day of MediSave can be used for rehabilitative care.

Figures are the Ministry of Health's published median actual bills for 2023 (inclusive of GST, before insurance reimbursement). Inpatient surgery uses the public Class A ward; rows marked "day surgery" use the unsubsidised day-surgery basis; stroke and pneumonia are inpatient bills without major complications. Two things worth understanding. First, government subsidies apply only to B2/C wards and the subsidised day-surgery tier — Class A carries no subsidy at all, and a citizen in a subsidised ward typically sees a bill of roughly a third to a quarter of the Class A figure for the same procedure. Second, these are bills for a single admission or procedure; for serious illness such as cancer, the full course of treatment (pre-operative investigations, surgery, chemotherapy and radiotherapy, follow-up) often runs to several times a single bill, so protection planning should be budgeted against the whole course of treatment.

What this table is really saying

For the same procedure, private runs roughly 1.5 to 3 times the unsubsidised public bill, and often more than ten times a subsidised ward — and for serious illness the full course of treatment multiplies that again. This is exactly what hospitalisation insurance is for: so that when you fall ill, you choose a hospital based on your condition rather than your bank balance.

08 · INSURANCE

Seven habits that make claims go smoothly

These are the lessons I've drawn from following up on countless claims as an adviser. Build these habits and reimbursement gets a great deal easier.

1

With group cover, get a referral before seeing a specialist

To claim specialist consultations on company group insurance you generally need a referral letter from a panel GP first, then a panel specialist. Going straight to a specialist may leave you unable to claim at all.

2

Ask for a memo before you leave

Accident claims need the doctor's diagnostic note. Ask for a memo while you are still there and uploading it to the app later is straightforward, with no going back for missing paperwork.

3

For planned surgery, arrange cashless admission in advance

Citizens and PRs generally have a Letter of Guarantee (LOG) limit at the hospital, so the bill goes straight to the insurer. If you exceed that limit, are a foreigner, or have pre-existing condition exclusions, apply for pre-authorisation two to three working days ahead — in most cases you can then be discharged without fronting a large sum.

4

Declare your health honestly when you apply

Chronic conditions, past surgery and long-term medication all need declaring. The rule is simple: if you would mention it to a doctor, declare it. This is what makes sure a claim actually pays out when the time comes.

5

Personal and group cover can stack

Whatever you still pay out of pocket after your personal hospitalisation plan settles can then be submitted to your company group plan, which can bring your out-of-pocket cost to zero. The order matters, so ask me first if you are unsure.

6

Critical illness cover has a waiting period

The usual rule is that beyond a 90-day waiting period, a diagnosis of a condition on the policy's defined list, together with meeting the survival period, is what qualifies for a claim. Read through the definitions list once you have bought — it is worth knowing where you stand.

7

Emergency hospitalisation overseas is claimable too

Hospitalisation insurance covers emergency admission abroad; where you have a choice, use a tertiary or equivalent-grade local hospital. Chinese-language medical records generally do not need separate translation.
